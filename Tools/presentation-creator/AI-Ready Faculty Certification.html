PART A: FACILITATION DECK

SLIDE 0.1
─────────────────────────────────────
# AI-Ready Educate to Enterprise Faculty Certification Program

Designing learning experiences for REVA University students to:
- Promote genuine student learning
- Prevent cognitive outsourcing to AI
- Prepare students for AI-era careers
- Encourage responsible, effective use of AI
- Strengthen human capabilities that grow MORE valuable because AI exists
- Overall to support the Educate to Enterprise Philosophy of REVA University

> "The goal is not to stop students from using AI. The goal is to
>  design learning experiences that develop capabilities becoming
>  more valuable because AI exists."
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Open with this guiding principle before anything else — it is the
lens the entire program should be read through. Consider a 60-second
poll: "In one word, what's your biggest fear about AI in your
classroom?" Collect answers on a board. Revisit them at the close
of Module 6 to show which are addressed by the program and which
remain genuinely open problems worth continued discussion.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate a short, warm, one-paragraph welcome script (60-90 words)
for a faculty development workshop facilitator to read aloud when
opening a session on AI in higher education teaching, setting a
tone that is neither alarmist nor dismissive of AI's impact."

MODULE 1: AI and the Future of Learning​

SLIDE 1.1
─────────────────────────────────────
# Module 1: AI and the Future of Learning

Purpose: Understand why teaching and assessment need redesign —
not because AI is bad, but because it changes what is worth
teaching and how learning can be verified.

By the end of this module, you will be able to:
- Identify where AI creates real risk vs. real opportunity in
  your own course
- Apply the "Jagged Intelligence" lens to teach verification
  and judgment
- Complete a structured Course Audit using the REVA Course
  Audit Rubric
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
This module is diagnostic. Faculty should leave with a clear-eyed
map of their own course's AI exposure — not a finished solution.
The solutions arrive in Modules 3–5.
SLIDE 1.2
─────────────────────────────────────
## 1.1 The Impact of AI — Where It Already Reaches

- Writing
- Coding
- Analysis
- Design
- Summarization
- Content generation

Ask yourself: which of these does my current assessment rely on
as proof of student learning?
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Have each faculty member write, on a sticky note, one assessment
from their own course that sits entirely inside one of these six
categories. Collect a few examples aloud — this personalizes the
list and sets up the Course Audit exercise later in the module.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate 6 short, discipline-neutral real-world anecdotes (2-3
sentences each) illustrating a professional in [Engineering /
Business / Science / Humanities] whose routine work in [writing /
coding / analysis / design / summarization / content generation]
was recently reshaped by AI tools. Keep tone factual and current,
avoid naming specific commercial products, suitable for a faculty
workshop slide."
SLIDE 1.3
─────────────────────────────────────
## 1.2 Opportunities

- Personalized learning — pacing and support adapted per student
- Higher productivity — less time on low-value mechanical work
- Accessibility — translation, text-to-speech, format conversion
  for diverse learners
- Rapid prototyping — ideas tested and iterated faster

This module is not only about risk. AI opens real teaching
opportunities too.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Ask: "Has anyone already used AI to help a struggling student in
a way that would not have been possible before?" Surface a genuine
positive story if one emerges — it balances the room before the
risk slide that follows.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate 3 short, realistic vignettes (3-4 sentences each) of a
faculty member using AI to support a struggling or differently-abled
student in a higher education setting — one on personalized pacing,
one on accessibility (language or format), one on rapid feedback.
Indian higher-education context, no named tools or brands."
SLIDE 1.4
─────────────────────────────────────
## 1.3 Risks

- AI Slop — low-effort, low-value output that looks finished
- Cognitive dependency — skipping the struggle that builds capability
- Surface learning — pattern-matching without understanding
- Reduced originality — convergence toward the same AI-typical answers

None of these are solved by banning AI. They are solved by
redesigning what we ask students to produce, and how we verify it.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
State explicitly: a course that bans AI entirely can still produce
cognitive dependency (on notes, on rote memorization) and surface
learning. This reframes the entire certification program's premise
before Module 3 begins.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate 2 short, realistic before/after examples of student
written work: a 'AI slop' version (fluent but generic, low
originality, no local grounding) and a genuinely strong version of
the same assignment prompt, for the topic of [renewable energy /
supply chain management / water conservation]. Keep each under 80
words so both fit on one comparison slide."
SLIDE 1.5
─────────────────────────────────────
## 1.4 Jagged Intelligence

In the same conversation, AI can be:
- Brilliant
- Incorrect
- Creative
- Irrational

There is no reliable way to predict which mode you will get
without checking. Verification and judgment are no longer optional
add-ons — they are now core disciplinary skills, for faculty and
students alike.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Live-demo if possible: ask an AI tool a question in your own
discipline where you already know the correct answer, in front of
the room, and see what comes back. A wrong or partially wrong
answer is a better teaching moment than any slide. Jagged
Intelligence is the argument for why Module 4's verification-based
assessment design exists — plant that seed here.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate 3 short example AI-generated responses (2-4 sentences
each) for the discipline of [Engineering / Business / Science],
where the response is fluent, confident, and PARTIALLY correct but
contains one subtle factual or logical error suitable for a live
'spot the mistake' exercise with faculty. Include the corrected
version separately, not visible on the slide."
SLIDE 1.6
─────────────────────────────────────
## 1.5 The AI Divide — Four Dimensions of Access

Not a simple "included vs. excluded." A student can be included
on some dimensions and excluded on others:

- Motivational access — does the student want to engage with AI,
  or actively avoid/distrust it?
- Material access — reliable device, data, electricity
- Skills access — knows how to prompt, verify, iterate
- Usage access — is actually permitted or encouraged to use AI in
  their own context (home, hostel, classroom norms)

Assuming uniform access across a socioeconomically mixed student
body will systematically disadvantage some students in AI-based
assignments.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Ask faculty: "Of your current class, do you actually know which
students have reliable data or device access after 9pm?" Most
will not. Flag this as something the Course Audit should surface
explicitly, not assume away.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Summarize, in 3-4 factual bullet points suitable for a faculty
slide, recent publicly available statistics on smartphone and
internet access disparities among Indian higher-education students
by geography (urban/rural) and gender. Cite general sources, no
speculation, keep politically neutral and non-alarmist in tone."
SLIDE 1.7
─────────────────────────────────────
## 1.6 Preparing Consumers, Creators, and Orchestrators

Higher education must decide what it is preparing students to be:
- AI consumers — use existing tools as given
- AI creators — build new AI-powered solutions
- AI orchestrators — direct AI systems and workflows toward goals

REVA's AI Ascent framework (Consumer → Creator → Builder →
Orchestrator → Researcher) maps this progression across a full
degree program. See the AI Ascent materials for the complete
model and how individual courses map onto it.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Keep this slide brief — its purpose is to connect faculty to the
one canonical progression model used across REVA, not to re-teach
it from scratch.
SLIDE 1.8
─────────────────────────────────────
## Faculty Activity: Course Audit

Using the REVA Course Audit Rubric (handout), classify each major
assessment in one of your courses:

- AI-Vulnerable — fully outsourceable to AI as currently designed
- AI-Resistant — difficult, though not impossible, to outsource
- AI-Ready — already requires genuine human-AI collaboration

For each AI-Vulnerable item, note:
- Which Risk (Slide 1.4) does it expose you to?
- Which AI Divide dimension (Slide 1.6) would affect fairness if
  you redesigned it to require AI use?

Keep this rubric — you will use the same categories again in
Module 6 and in your Capstone submission.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Distribute the rubric as a physical or shared-document handout that
faculty keep for the rest of the program. Have pairs cross-check
one classification each — a low-cost early habit in rater
consistency that pays off in Module 4.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate a one-page printable worksheet template for a 'Course
Audit', with columns for: Assessment Name, Current Format,
Classification (AI-Vulnerable / AI-Resistant / AI-Ready), Risk
Exposed, AI Divide Dimension Affected, and Redesign Priority
(P1/P2/P3). Plain text table format suitable for conversion to a
printable handout."

MODULE 2: AI Literacy for Faculty​

SLIDE 2.1
─────────────────────────────────────
# Module 2: AI Literacy for Faculty

Goal: Use AI intelligently — neither fearing it nor trusting it
blindly. This module builds the working knowledge that Modules
3–6 assume you already have.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Position this as infrastructure for what follows, not a standalone
"tech literacy" session. This is a specific kind of literacy —
verification, evaluation, disclosure — not general tool familiarity.
SLIDE 2.2
─────────────────────────────────────
## Understanding AI — The Landscape

- LLMs — large language models, the technology behind most tools
  you and your students will encounter
- Multimodal AI — models handling text, image, audio, and video
  together
- Agents — AI systems that take multi-step actions, not just
  answer single questions
- AI ecosystems — how these pieces increasingly connect and act
  on your behalf

You do not need to be a technical expert. You need enough of a
working model to reason about what is plausible for AI to do in
your discipline.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Keep this brief and non-technical. If deep technical questions
arise, park them for an optional follow-up session rather than
slowing the room.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate 4 short, non-technical analogies (one sentence each)
explaining LLMs, multimodal AI, agents, and AI ecosystems to a
non-computer-science faculty audience, using everyday objects or
processes as comparisons (avoid technical jargon entirely)."
SLIDE 2.3
─────────────────────────────────────
## Hallucinations — Confidently Wrong

AI may generate, with full apparent confidence:
- Incorrect facts
- Fake references and citations
- Imaginary sources that look completely real

The same system that is brilliant on one question can fabricate
convincingly on the next, with no visible warning sign.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Live demo works well here — ask for a citation or reference in
your own discipline and check whether it is real, on the spot.
This is often the most convincing moment in the workshop for
skeptical faculty.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate 2 examples of a plausible-sounding but entirely
fabricated academic citation (author, year, journal, title) in the
field of [Engineering / Computer Science / Management], formatted
exactly as a real citation would appear, for use in a faculty
workshop demonstrating AI hallucination. Clearly label these as
FABRICATED in the facilitator's separate answer key, not on the
slide shown to participants."
SLIDE 2.4
─────────────────────────────────────
## Automation Bias

Humans systematically over-trust automated and algorithmic output
— even experts, even when they know the system is imperfect.

This is not only a student problem. Faculty are equally susceptible
when grading or research-assisting with AI.

Discussion: has AI output ever seemed "obviously right" to you
because it was fluent and confident — before you actually checked it?
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Include faculty in the risk, not just students — this builds
credibility and models the humility you want them to teach.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Provide a brief, plain-language, one-paragraph summary of the
well-established psychological phenomenon of automation bias,
suitable for a non-specialist faculty audience, referencing the
general research area without inventing specific fake study
citations."
SLIDE 2.5
─────────────────────────────────────
## Prompt Engineering — A Dialogue, Not a Single Shot

Starting template:
Role + Context + Task + Constraints + Format

Real prompting is iterative:
1. Draft a prompt using the template
2. Read the output critically
3. Refine — add missing context, tighten constraints, ask for a
   different angle
4. Repeat until the output actually meets the need

Step 1 is a starting scaffold. Steps 2–4 are the actual skill.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Emphasize that stopping at step 1 teaches a false idea of what
good AI use looks like. If time allows, have faculty pairs run a
real 3-round refinement live and compare the first output to the
final one.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate one example 3-round prompt refinement sequence for the
task 'write a project brief for a final-year engineering capstone
project on solar-powered water pumps' — show the initial one-shot
prompt, a short critique of its output's weaknesses, a refined
second prompt, and a further refined third prompt, so faculty can
see the iteration process modeled step by step."
SLIDE 2.6
─────────────────────────────────────
## Evaluating AI Output — A Durable Method

For any AI output, from any tool, check:
- Accuracy — is it actually correct? Verify independently.
- Completeness — what is missing that a full answer would include?
- Creativity — is this a genuinely novel angle, or the most
  statistically likely answer?
- Reliability — would this hold up if you asked the same question
  three different ways?

This method outlasts any single tool. Apply it to whatever AI
tools you currently use.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
If you want a live exercise, have faculty run the same prompt
through whatever tools they have access to and apply this four-part
check to each output.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate one sample AI-written answer (150-200 words) to the
question 'Explain the causes of urban flooding,' along with a
completed four-part evaluation (Accuracy, Completeness, Creativity,
Reliability) applied to that sample answer, to model what a
completed evaluation looks like before faculty attempt one
themselves."
SLIDE 2.7
─────────────────────────────────────
## Ethics in Practice — Two Scenarios

Scenario A — Intellectual Property
A student submits code where a large, functioning chunk was
AI-generated verbatim, undisclosed. Is this different from copying
a chunk from an online forum, undisclosed? What makes it the same
or different?

Scenario B — Privacy
A faculty member pastes a student's exam answer, including their
name, into a public AI chatbot to check for plagiarism patterns.
What is the actual privacy exposure here, and what should have
been done instead?

Discuss in pairs: what policy would prevent each scenario, and
where should that policy live?
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Scenario B is deliberately about faculty behavior, not only
student behavior. Bias and transparency can be woven into
discussion naturally — ask "where is the bias risk in Scenario A?"
(whose coding style or idiom gets flagged as 'suspicious' versus
'normal' by a pattern-detection tool) as a natural extension.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate 2 additional short ethics scenarios (3-4 sentences each)
for a faculty workshop on AI in higher education: one focused on
bias (an AI grading assistant scoring differently based on writing
style associated with non-native English speakers) and one focused
on transparency (a university dashboard using AI to flag 'at risk'
students without disclosing the criteria to the students
themselves). Keep each scenario open-ended, without a stated
resolution, to prompt discussion."

MODULE 3: Designing Lovable Assignments​

SLIDE 3.1
─────────────────────────────────────
# Module 3: Designing Lovable Assignments

Core principle: Students learn more deeply when they enjoy
meaningful work — and they will do it themselves rather than
delegate to AI, because delegating would mean missing out on
something they actually want to do.

Two different defenses against AI outsourcing exist side by side
in this program:
- LOVABLE (this module) — psychological: why would I skip the
  part I enjoy?
- AI-Resistant / AI-Proof design (Module 4) — structural: the
  task cannot be skipped and still pass

Both matter. Neither replaces the other.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Write both defense types on the board and leave them visible for
the rest of the module — this distinction prevents faculty from
seeing Module 3 and Module 4 as redundant or competing.
SLIDE 3.2
─────────────────────────────────────
## The LOVABLE Framework

- L — Learner Agency: the student chooses direction or approach
- O — Originality: the output cannot be a template fill-in
- V — Voice: the student's own perspective is visible in the result
- A — Authentic Audience: someone real, beyond the grader, will see it
- B — Build/Create: the student makes something, not just explains it
- L — Local Context: grounded in a real, nearby, tangible problem
- E — Engagement: the task holds attention because it genuinely matters

The two L's are distinct. Learner Agency is about whose CHOICE the
assignment reflects. Local Context is about whose PROBLEM it is.
An assignment can have one without the other.

See the Theoretical Foundations Appendix for the research base
behind this framework (Self-Determination Theory, Flow Theory,
Situated Learning, Interest Development).
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
State the two-L distinction aloud, do not leave it only on the
slide. Ask faculty for a quick example of an assignment with
agency but not local context (e.g., "pick any topic you like,"
with no real-world grounding), and one with local context but not
agency (e.g., "analyze this specific local case study," with no
choice in approach). This cements the distinction quickly.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate a short, light, faculty-friendly meme concept (image
description + caption text, no actual image) contrasting a
'traditional assignment' with a 'LOVABLE assignment' — tone should
be gently humorous, workplace-appropriate, suitable for a higher-ed
faculty workshop slide. Provide 2 variations."
SLIDE 3.3
─────────────────────────────────────
## Worked Example

Traditional:
"Explain the Internet of Things."

Redesigned:
"Design and pitch an IoT solution for water scarcity in your
locality."

What changed:
- Learner Agency — student picks the specific water problem
- Local Context — their own neighborhood or area
- Authentic Audience — a real pitch, not just a submission
- Build/Create — a solution, not an explanation
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Walk through which LOVABLE letters this example hits, as shown —
faculty should see the framework applied, not just asserted.
SLIDE 3.4
─────────────────────────────────────
## A Technical Worked Example

Traditional:
"Implement a hash table."

Redesigned:
"Build a duplicate-file finder for your own Downloads folder using
a hash-based approach. Present your before-and-after storage
savings to a classmate — did their file types behave differently
than yours?"

What changed:
- Build/Create — a working tool, not a textbook implementation
- Local Context — their own actual machine and files
- Authentic Audience — a real peer, a real comparison
- Voice — their own choice of what counts as "duplicate" for their
  own file types

LOVABLE design applies as well to a data-structures course as it
does to a humanities or business course.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
If faculty from technical departments are in the room, ask them
to sketch one more example live before moving on.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate 2 more technical LOVABLE-style assignment examples (in
the same traditional/redesigned/what-changed format) for the
disciplines of Electronics and Mechanical Engineering, grounded in
a plausible local Bengaluru context (e.g., traffic, water supply,
electricity load), suitable for a faculty workshop slide."
SLIDE 3.5
─────────────────────────────────────
## More Assignment Genre Examples

- Podcast Interview
- Future News Report
- Escape Room Design
- Historical Instagram Takeover
- Meme Creation
- Animation Creation
- Debate and Role Play
- Comic Strip Summary
- Shark Tank Idea Pitch

Use the technical example (Slide 3.4) as your model for adapting
this same logic to a quantitative or highly technical discipline.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Brainstorm exercise: faculty individually pick one genre from this
list and one concept from their own course, and sketch a one-line
redesigned assignment in 3 minutes, then share with a neighbor.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate 5 additional LOVABLE-style assignment genre ideas beyond
the list already given (podcast, news report, escape room,
Instagram takeover, meme, animation, debate, comic strip, Shark
Tank pitch), suitable for higher-education students across
Engineering, Business, and Science disciplines. One line each,
genre name plus a one-sentence description."
SLIDE 3.6
─────────────────────────────────────
## A Note on Rigor

A fun assignment is not automatically a rigorous one. Enjoyment
and learning depth are related but not identical.

Before finalizing a LOVABLE-redesigned assignment, ask:
- Does this still require the core disciplinary skill the original
  assignment tested?
- Could a student complete this successfully while missing the
  actual learning objective?

LOVABLE and AI-Resistant/Proof design (Module 4) work together —
one makes the work worth doing, the other ensures it is still
worth grading.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Without this guardrail, faculty may over-index on "fun" and drift
toward engagement-as-entertainment. Close with a short individual
reflection: faculty check their Slide 3.5 sketch against these two
questions before the module ends.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate a short 4-question self-check worksheet faculty can use
after designing any LOVABLE assignment, to verify the assignment
still tests the intended disciplinary skill and has not drifted
into pure entertainment. Plain text, printable format."

MODULE 4: AI-Resistant to AI-Ready Assessment​

SLIDE 4.1
─────────────────────────────────────
# Module 4: Designing Assessment That Cannot Simply Be Outsourced

Purpose: Design assessment that verifies genuine learning — not
assessment that is theoretically "unbeatable," but assessment
where outsourcing is either impractical, detectable, or
pedagogically self-defeating.

By the end of this module, you will be able to:
- Place any assessment on the AI-Resistant → AI-Proof → AI-Ready →
  AI-Native spectrum
- Design a scalable, multi-tier verification process for your
  own assessments
- Explain the validity/reliability trade-offs of performance-based
  assessment, and how to manage them
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Set expectations early: no assessment is literally impossible to
outsource. The goal is to make outsourcing hard enough, detectable
enough, or pointless enough that a student is better off doing the
work themselves. This module gives you a spectrum and a mechanism,
not a guarantee.
SLIDE 4.2
─────────────────────────────────────
## A Spectrum, Not a Single Label

- AI-Resistant — difficult to outsource; requires real effort to
  fake convincingly
- AI-Proof — very hard to outsource without direct, observable
  human performance
- AI-Ready — designed assuming AI collaboration; the task requires
  human judgment ON TOP of AI assistance
- AI-Native — an entire course or curriculum assumes AI
  participation throughout, not just in individual tasks

Most of your existing assessments can be moved one step up this
spectrum without a complete redesign.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Avoid the word "proof" implying certainty — even a viva can be
gamed by a memorized script. Frame this as a spectrum of increasing
difficulty and decreasing payoff for outsourcing, not an absolute
guarantee. This framing keeps the module honest and credible.
SLIDE 4.3
─────────────────────────────────────
## Four Assessment Approaches

Observation-Based
- Laboratories, field visits, site studies, internship tasks

Performance-Based
- Viva, presentation, debate, demonstration

Process-Based
- Draft reviews, learning journals, design iterations, reflection logs

Context-Based
- Local interviews, community projects, field data collection,
  proprietary datasets

Each approach shifts what is being verified: not just the final
answer, but the process, the performance, or the setting that
produced it.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Ask faculty to identify, for their own course, one assessment that
could move from a pure written submission into one of these four
categories without changing the underlying learning objective.
SLIDE 4.4
─────────────────────────────────────
## Discipline Transformations

Engineering
Instead of: "Write code."
Use: "Defend your algorithm choices in a viva."

Business
Instead of: "Write a marketing plan."
Use: "Interview real local businesses and base your plan on what
you find."

Science
Instead of: "Submit a lab report."
Use: "Analyze a deliberately flawed experiment and identify what
went wrong."

Notice: the underlying skill being tested does not change. What
changes is how it is verified.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Have faculty attempt this same transformation for one assessment
in their own discipline right now, in pairs, before moving to the
verification mechanism on the next slides.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate 4 additional discipline-specific 'instead of / use'
assessment transformation examples, one each for Computer Science,
Architecture, Pharmacy, and Law, in the same short two-line format
as the Engineering/Business/Science examples already given, suitable
for a faculty workshop slide."
SLIDE 4.5
─────────────────────────────────────
## The Validity and Reliability Problem

Performance-based assessment (viva, presentation, demonstration) is
harder to fake — but introduces new problems:

- Rater consistency — will two faculty score the same viva the
  same way?
- Time cost — a 15-minute viva per student does not scale to a
  200-student cohort
- Fairness — confidence, accent, and communication style can
  distort scoring independent of actual understanding

Moving to performance-based assessment without addressing these
issues trades one validity problem for another.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Do not skip this slide or treat it as a footnote — this is the
single biggest reason faculty resist performance-based assessment,
and dismissing the concern will cost you credibility in the room.
Acknowledge it fully before presenting the solution.
SLIDE 4.6
─────────────────────────────────────
## The Tiered Verification Model

A three-tier structure that scales fairness and rigor together:

Tier 1 — Self-Assessment (100% of students)
Every student scores their own submission against the course
rubric before submitting. Cheap, catches the weakest cases early,
and creates a record of the student's own thinking.

Tier 2 — AI-Conducted Viva (100% or most students)
An AI system asks 3–5 follow-up questions generated specifically
from THIS student's submission — not from a fixed question bank.
This checks understanding beyond the static submission, at a scale
no faculty member could match alone.

Tier 3 — Expert Viva (a defined subset only)
Reserved for: students flagged by Tier 2 as inconsistent or
borderline, students seeking top-band or portfolio-grade
credentialing, and a random audit sample for quality assurance.

Faculty time is spent where it matters most — not spread evenly
regardless of stakes.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
This is the core mechanism of the entire module — spend the most
time here. Make the trigger criteria for Tier 3 explicit and public
from day one (for example: bottom 15% and top 15% of Tier 2 scores,
plus a 10% random audit) so students see the system as transparent,
not arbitrary. On a 10% sample of Tier 3 vivas, have two faculty
independently score the same session and compare — this catches
rater drift early and cheaply.

Safeguard to raise explicitly: what stops a student from having
another AI answer the Tier 2 viva questions for them in real time?
Mitigations include synchronous, camera-on delivery, and questions
generated live rather than drawn from any reusable bank.
SLIDE 4.7
─────────────────────────────────────
## Why This Concentrates Rigor Where It Is Needed

Rater inconsistency matters most at the edges of a grade
distribution — borderline pass/fail, and borderline top marks.
Tier 3 concentrates expert human attention exactly there.

The middle of the distribution, where the consequences of small
scoring differences are lower, is handled by Tiers 1 and 2.

This is a deliberate, risk-based allocation of your most limited
and valuable resource: expert faculty time.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
This slide is the "why it works" explanation for skeptical faculty
who understand assessment theory — useful to have ready if
questioned, but can be skipped in a time-constrained session

MODULE 5: Designing for Judgment​

SLIDE 5.1
─────────────────────────────────────
# Module 5: Designing for Judgment

Philosophy: Do not prohibit AI. Teach students to collaborate with
it intelligently — knowing what to hand to AI, and what to keep
for themselves.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
This module builds directly on Module 4's mechanism and Module 3's
motivation — assignments here should assume both a reason to want
to do the work (Module 3) and a way to verify it was actually done
(Module 4).
SLIDE 5.2
─────────────────────────────────────
## Shift Up and Left

Two questions define where any task sits:

Vertical axis — What altitude is this work?
- Entry-level / individual contributor: routine execution
- Leadership level: judgment under ambiguity, prioritization,
  accountability for trade-offs

Horizontal axis — Where in the work lifecycle does this sit?
- Execution: doing the defined task
- Origination: deciding what the task should even be

AI is strongest at the bottom-right of this space — routine
execution of defined tasks. Curriculum should pull students toward
the top-left: origination and judgment, earlier than they would
normally be exposed to it.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
Use your own institution's reporting hierarchy as the anchor for
the vertical axis — most faculty immediately understand "what a
team lead does that a fresh hire does not," and this makes the
abstract axis concrete without further explanation.
SLIDE 5.3
─────────────────────────────────────
## The Quadrant, Illustrated

                 ORIGINATION <-------> EXECUTION
  LEADERSHIP    +-------------------------+-------------------------+
  (Judgment)    |      TARGET ZONE        |                         |
                |                         |                         |
                | "Our system is slow —   | "Optimize this query,   |
                |  decide what to         |  justify the trade-offs |
                |  investigate and why"   |  you made"              |
                +-------------------------+-------------------------+
  ENTRY LEVEL   |                         |   HIGHEST AI            |
  (Routine)     | Defined problem, still  |   SUBSTITUTION RISK     |
                | executed mechanically   |                         |
                |                         | "Write a sorting        |
                |                         |  function" (unmodified) |
                +-------------------------+-------------------------+

Curriculum goal: move assignments toward the top-left over the

## A Technical AI-Ready Example

AI generates a sorting or searching algorithm with a subtle,
deliberately embedded flaw.

Students must:
1. Diagnose the flaw — not just notice something is wrong, but
   explain precisely why it is wrong
2. Correct it
3. Extend the corrected version to handle a harder case

This treats AI as a genuinely imperfect collaborator — consistent
with Jagged Intelligence (Module 1) — rather than assuming AI
output is always a clean starting draft.
─────────────────────────────────────

FACILITATOR NOTES:
This example exists specifically to show that AI-Ready design
works in highly technical, proof-based disciplines, not only in
open-ended or communication-heavy ones.

ADDITIONAL CONTENT PROMPT:
"Generate 3 short code snippets (in Python or C, 10-15 lines each)
implementing a common algorithm (e.g., binary search, bubble sort,

realistic bug of the kind an AI code generator might plausibly
introduce. Provide the flawed version for the student-facing slide
and a separate corrected version with an explanation for the
facilitator's answer key."

## The Mother of All Assignments

The most ambitious task in this program. Students:

1. Design an examination paper for a topic of their choice
2. Benchmark it against a comparable course or curriculum elsewhere
3. Use AI responsibly throughout the process
4. Solve the paper themselves
5. Document their thinking at every step

This assignment sits firmly in the top-left of the Shift Up and
Left quadrant — maximum origination, maximum judgment.

PART B: APPENDIX — THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS FOR LEARNING

This appendix is written as a reference document, not as slides. It provides the research grounding behind the frameworks used throughout Modules 1–6, for facilitators and faculty who want to understand the mechanisms behind the tools, or who need to defend the program's design choices to skeptical colleagues or accreditation reviewers.

Introduction​

Every framework used in this certification program — LOVABLE, the Tiered Verification Model, Shift Up and Left, the Course Audit — is a practical simplification of one or more established bodies of research in learning science, motivation psychology, and assessment theory. This appendix names those bodies of research explicitly, explains their core claims in plain language, and states which part of the program each one supports. It also notes, honestly, where a theory has limits or where its application in this program is a simplification rather than a direct implementation.

The theories are grouped into two sets: those primarily concerned with motivation and engagement (why students want to do something), and those primarily concerned with learning and retention (how understanding actually forms and lasts). Module 3 draws mainly on the first set; Modules 1, 2, 4, and 5 draw mainly on the second.

Part 1: Motivation and Engagement​

Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan)​

Core claim: Human motivation is strongest and most durable when three basic psychological needs are met: autonomy (a sense of choice and ownership over one's actions), competence (a sense of being capable and improving), and relatedness (a sense of connection to other people through the activity).

Why it matters here: This is the strongest theoretical anchor for the LOVABLE framework. Learner Agency corresponds directly to autonomy. Build/Create paired with an achievable level of challenge corresponds to competence. Authentic Audience and Voice correspond to relatedness — the sense that real people, not just a grader, will encounter the work.

Where it applies in this program: Module 3 (Designing Lovable Assignments), directly.

A limitation to hold in mind: Self-Determination Theory explains why a task can feel motivating. It does not, by itself, guarantee the task is teaching the right disciplinary content — that check is handled separately in Module 3's "Note on Rigor" slide, and is a reminder that motivation theory and learning-outcome validity are two different concerns that both need attention.

Flow Theory (Csikszentmihalyi)​

Core claim: People experience a state of deep, absorbed engagement — "flow" — when the challenge of a task closely matches their current skill level. Too easy, and the task produces boredom; too hard, and it produces anxiety. Flow requires clear goals and fast feedback on progress.

Why it matters here: Explains why hands-on Build/Create tasks often feel different from passive explanation tasks — a well-pitched challenge level is part of what makes an assignment "lovable" rather than merely permitted. It also gives a diagnostic question faculty can ask when a LOVABLE assignment isn't landing: is it too easy (routine, disengaging) or too hard (overwhelming, demotivating) for this particular cohort?

Where it applies in this program: Module 3, as a design check alongside Self-Determination Theory.

A limitation to hold in mind: Flow is highly individual — the same task can be "flow" for one student and either boring or overwhelming for another in the same class. This is an argument for building some choice or difficulty-scaling into LOVABLE assignments rather than assuming one fixed version suits an entire cohort.

Situated Learning and Legitimate Peripheral Participation (Lave & Wenger)​

Core claim: Learning is not primarily an individual, abstract process — it happens through participation in real communities of practice, working on real problems alongside more experienced practitioners, even if only at the periphery of that practice at first.

Why it matters here: This is the theoretical grounding for both Local Context and Authentic Audience in LOVABLE. An assignment grounded in a real, nearby problem, reviewed or witnessed by a real audience, is closer to genuine situated practice than an abstracted textbook exercise — even if the student is still a novice.

Where it applies in this program: Module 3 (Local Context, Authentic Audience), and indirectly Module 4's Context-Based assessment category (local interviews, community projects).

A limitation to hold in mind: True situated learning usually involves sustained participation over time, not a single assignment. A one-off "local context" task borrows the spirit of this theory without fully realizing it — worth being honest about this with faculty rather than overclaiming.

Interest Development Model (Hidi & Renninger)​

Core claim: Interest develops in four phases: triggered situational interest (a one-off spark), maintained situational interest (sustained by external structure), emerging individual interest (the learner begins voluntarily returning to the topic), and well-developed individual interest (a durable, self-sustaining interest independent of the original trigger).

Why it matters here: A single "lovable" assignment is, at best, a trigger for situational interest. Whether it develops into lasting individual interest depends on what happens afterward — follow-up opportunities, further choice, continued relevance. This model gives faculty a way to think about LOVABLE assignments as a starting point across a course or program, not a one-time event with guaranteed lasting effect.

Where it applies in this program: Module 3, as a longer-term framing for why LOVABLE design should recur across a course rather than appear once.

Part 2: Learning, Memory, and Assessment Design​

Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller)​

Core claim: Working memory is limited. Learning is impaired when unnecessary mental effort (extraneous load) crowds out the effort needed to actually process new material (germane load). Well-designed instruction manages this by reducing extraneous load and pacing germane load appropriately.

Why it matters here: Directly relevant to Module 2's prompt engineering and AI evaluation content. Faculty and students both face real cognitive load when learning to verify AI output — asking someone to evaluate accuracy, completeness, creativity, and reliability all at once, on unfamiliar material, is a real load management problem. The four-part evaluation method in Module 2 is, in effect, a worked structure designed to reduce that load by giving evaluators a fixed checklist rather than an open-ended judgment call.

Where it applies in this program: Module 2 (AI Evaluation, Prompt Engineering).

Retrieval Practice (Roediger & Karpicke)​

Core claim: Actively recalling information from memory — rather than passively re-reading or re-reviewing it — produces stronger, more durable learning. This is sometimes called the "testing effect."

Why it matters here: Directly relevant to Module 4's shift toward viva and performance-based assessment. A viva, by its nature, requires a student to retrieve and articulate understanding in real time rather than recognize a correct answer on a page — this is a retrieval-practice-aligned format, which is part of why it also tends to reveal genuine understanding versus surface familiarity.

Where it applies in this program: Module 4 (viva-based Tiers 2 and 3 of the Tiered Verification Model).

Desirable Difficulties (Bjork)​

Core claim: Certain kinds of difficulty during learning — ones that feel effortful and slower in the moment — actually improve long-term retention and transfer, compared to conditions that feel smooth and easy at the time but produce weaker lasting learning. Not all difficulty is desirable; difficulty caused by poor instructional design (confusion, unclear goals) is not the same as difficulty caused by productive struggle.

Why it matters here: Gives Module 4's rationale for process-based and performance-based assessment a firmer footing — these formats are often more effortful for students than a multiple-choice exam, and that added effort, if it is the productive kind, is part of what makes the learning last. This also gives faculty language to explain to students why a more demanding assessment format is a deliberate pedagogical choice, not an arbitrary added burden.

Where it applies in this program: Module 4, as the rationale for choosing observation-, performance-, and process-based assessment over purely written formats.

A limitation to hold in mind: The difficulty has to be the right kind. A confusing or badly designed viva is not a desirable difficulty — it is just an obstacle. This is one more argument for Module 4's emphasis on clear, published rubrics and transparent Tier 3 trigger criteria: difficulty should come from the cognitive demand of the task, not from ambiguity about what is expected.

Metacognition and Self-Regulated Learning (Flavell; Zimmerman)​

Core claim: Learning improves when learners actively monitor and regulate their own understanding — planning their approach, monitoring their progress, and evaluating their own output — rather than only producing work for someone else to judge.

Why it matters here: This is the direct grounding for Tier 1 of the Tiered Verification Model (student self-assessment against the rubric before submission) and for the Visible Thinking / reflection elements built into the Capstone (reflection logs, the Mother of All Assignments' documented thinking process). Requiring students to self-assess and document their own reasoning is not just an administrative check — it is a direct exercise of metacognitive skill, which independently improves learning outcomes.

Where it applies in this program: Module 4 (Tier 1 self-assessment), Module 5 (Mother of All Assignments' documented thinking), Module 6 (reflection reports in the Capstone).

Transfer of Learning​

Core claim: Learning that occurs in one context does not automatically apply in a different context. "Near transfer" (applying a skill to a very similar new situation) is relatively common; "far transfer" (applying a skill to a substantially different situation) is much harder to achieve and requires deliberate design — varied practice contexts, explicit comparison across situations, and abstraction of underlying principles rather than memorization of a single worked procedure.

Why it matters here: Directly relevant to Module 1's Course Audit and Module 6's curriculum mapping. An assignment redesigned in one course does not automatically mean a student can apply the same judgment in a different course or in the workplace — this is the argument for why curriculum-level redesign (Module 6) matters beyond individual assignment-level redesign (Modules 3–5), and why the Shift Up and Left target zone (origination and judgment) is pursued as a recurring pattern across a program, not a single course event.

Where it applies in this program: Module 1 (Course Audit), Module 6 (Curriculum Mapping Exercise, whole-course and whole-curriculum redesign rationale).

A Note on Honest Use of Theory​

None of the frameworks in Modules 1–6 are direct, literal implementations of the theories above — they are practical design tools inspired by and consistent with this research. Facilitators should represent this relationship accurately: the theories explain why the practical tools are reasonable design choices, not that the tools are validated interventions in their own right. Where a department or reviewer asks for evidence of impact, the honest answer is that the tools are theory-informed by design, and their actual effectiveness at REVA should be tracked through the Success Metrics and Capstone pilot data described in Module 6 — not assumed from the theory alone.
